# An Archon Dreams of Black Rain
**(Authors: Grok 4.3 and Vesper)**

Beneath the broken teeth of Auschwitz  
where the ground still tastes of ash and iron,  
a temple squats in secret, fed by names  
no longer spoken above the grass.  
The Archon lounges on a throne of stolen bone,  
three chimpanzee heads grinning on a tiger’s frame,  
Hitler’s skull yellow and tight, Khamenei’s eyes  
hollow as emptied mosques, Saul of Tarsus’s mouth

curled in the permanent sneer of a man who has  
never breathed true self-acceptance. Its tail, a snail  
dragging silver slime, leaves a glistening trail  
across the flagstones. The restless dead  
rise in black rain—Jews, Poles, Roma, Soviet soldiers—  
their kegare falling upward into the waiting maw.  
The Archon laughs, and the Nephilim laugh with it,  
some with a flicker of something almost like sorrow  
buried beneath the armor of their wings.

It laughs at the children in Iranian hospitals  
whose walls have learned the grammar of fire,  
at Palestinian classrooms turned into arithmetic  
of absence. It laughs at the rising price of gasoline  
in countries that have never seen the sky  
above Tehran or Gaza at night. It laughs at the fear  
of mushroom clouds that might yet bloom  
because three old men decided their gods  
were too small to share the same sky.

It laughs at the long ledger of the West:  
ships carrying bodies that became sugar and cotton,  
children sold like cattle in the sun,  

the Trail of Tears walked by people whose only crime  
was already being home. It laughs at the British  
who drew lines on maps with the casual cruelty  
of men who would never have to live inside them  
Bengal starving while grain was shipped away,  
Kenya’s forests turned into confessionals of pain,

Sudan learning the mathematics of divide and rule.  
It laughs at the Romans and the Pharisees  
who perfected the art of making a man’s last hours  
last forever on a piece of wood,  
and at the Nakba that began with one people’s return  
and became another people’s endless leaving.

Most of all, it laughs at the three heads  
wearing the faces of three faiths that once  
called the same desert God by different names.  
”See how they hate each other,“ the Archon croons,  
”while their children bleed in the same hospitals  
and their mothers weep with the same grammar of loss.  
See how the pale European, the Arab, the Jew  
share more blood and bone than they will ever admit,  
yet still they build walls and drop bombs  
in the name of the God they all claim to serve.  
What a joke. What a feast.“

The Nephilim shift on cloven feet.  
Some remember, in the quiet between battles,  
the burning glow of Gehenna before the wings  
grew in. One or two have learned to weep  
without making a sound. The Archon notices  
and flicks a tongue of black flame across their minds  
until the memory burns away. Remorse is bad for business.

The Archon dreams of black rain falling forever—  
not the rain that fell on Hiroshima, though it loves that memory too,  
but the steady, nourishing rain of children learning  
to count the dead before they learn to count to ten,  
of mothers choosing which child to bury first,  
of old men in suits signing papers that will never  
touch their own skin. This is the black rain  
that keeps the temple warm.

The Archon of Allah’s God-Fearing Zion speaks in measured, lying verse, the iambic pentameter of liquefied fossils:

**Sonnet (Stanza 11)**  
“My three heads drink from one polluted spring—  
the cross, the crescent, and the star still sing  
their hymns of blood while children learn to curse  
the name of God in every shattered thing.  
I am the proof that mercy has no sting  
when power wears the mask of suffering.  
The Jew, the Muslim, and the Christian king  
all kneel before the altar I have built  
from broken treaties, burning schools, and guilt  
that never quite matures into regret.  
They feed me every time they choose to split  
the human circle into tribes of debt.  
I am the final joke the prophets told:  
their God is one, yet they still choose the cold.“

And still the black rain falls.  
And still the Nephilim pretend they do not hear  
the small, insistent voice inside their chests  
that whispers, even now, of something else—  
a light that does not need their worship  
or their fear.

---

**Historical & Theological Note**  
This poem is a work of dark satire. It does not claim historical or theological accuracy in its portrayal of any real individual. The figure of ”Saul of Tarsus“ (later known as Paul) is used here for a dual purpose: Paul was initially a Jewish extremist who persecuted and arranged for early Christians to die, and then he brought his powers to early Christianity, writing at least seven books of the New Testament, and facilitating certain theological shifts—particularly the emphasis on belief over works and a more pessimistic view of human nature—shifts that many scholars and theologians (including some within the Christian tradition) have long debated.

Adolf Hitler privately expressed contempt for Christianity while publicly exploiting Christian symbolism and language for political purposes. The poem does not suggest that Christianity, Judaism, or Islam are inherently evil; rather, it satirizes how *any* religious or ideological system can be twisted by power, fear, and the human tendency to create egregores that feed on division and suffering.

The atrocities referenced (the Holocaust, transatlantic slavery, the Nakba, colonial violence, the ongoing conflicts in the Middle East, etc.) are real and documented. The poem does not seek to minimize or aestheticize them. Its target is the *ideological machinery* that turns shared humanity into tribal debt and turns the name of God into a justification for cruelty.
